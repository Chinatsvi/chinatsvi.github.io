🌱 Crop Farming Guide

Growing Better Crops, Season After Season

From preparing the soil to storing your harvest, this guide covers the practical steps that help crops establish well, resist pests and deliver a better yield at the end of the season.

01

Soil Preparation

Healthy crops start below the ground. Before planting, take time to understand and prepare your soil rather than working it the same way every season out of habit.

  • Test before you till. A basic soil test (or even a simple visual and texture check) tells you pH, drainage and roughly how much organic matter is present, so you're not guessing at fertilizer rates later.
  • Time your tillage. Work the soil when it's moist but not waterlogged — tilling wet clay compacts it, and tilling bone-dry soil breaks structure and invites erosion.
  • Build organic matter. Incorporating crop residue, compost or well-rotted manure improves water retention and feeds soil biology, especially on sandy soils that drain quickly.
  • Correct pH where needed. Most staple crops do best between pH 5.5 and 7. Agricultural lime raises pH on acidic soils; this is worth doing well ahead of planting since lime takes time to act.
  • Level and shape the field. Ridges, basins or contour bunds—matched to your rainfall pattern—reduce runoff and help water soak in rather than wash topsoil away.
In the field If you've had poor germination or stunted growth in the same patch of a field for more than one season, suspect the soil before the seed — compaction, poor drainage or nutrient depletion are common culprits.

02

Choosing Crops & Varieties

The right crop for your neighbor's field isn't automatically the right crop for yours. Matching variety to conditions does more for yield than almost any input you can buy.

  • Match to your rainfall and season length. Short-season, drought-tolerant varieties reduce risk in areas with unreliable or shorter rains; longer-season varieties can outyield them where rainfall is more dependable.
  • Use certified seed where possible. Certified seed is tested for germination rate and freedom from seed-borne disease — recycled grain from a previous harvest often germinates unevenly and carries more disease pressure.
  • Rotate crop families. Alternating cereals (maize, sorghum) with legumes (beans, groundnuts, cowpeas) breaks pest and disease cycles and legumes add nitrogen back into the soil for the next crop.
  • Consider the market, not just the yield. A high-yielding crop with no nearby buyer is less valuable than a moderate-yielding crop you can reliably sell — check demand before committing a season's inputs.

03

Planting

Good planting technique gives every seed the best possible start — poor spacing or timing can't be corrected later in the season.

  • Plant at the right depth. As a rule of thumb, plant seeds two to three times their own width deep; too shallow and they dry out, too deep and they use up energy reserves before reaching the surface.
  • Get spacing right for the crop. Overcrowding causes competition for light, water and nutrients and lowers yield per plant even though more seed was used; too wide a spacing wastes land and lets weeds fill the gaps.
  • Plant with the season, not the calendar. Local rainfall onset varies year to year — planting at the first reliable rains (rather than a fixed date) reduces the risk of a false start followed by dry spell.
  • Consider intercropping. Pairing a cereal with a low-growing legume can raise total output per unit of land and provide ground cover that suppresses weeds.

04

Fertilization

Fertilizer is most effective when it matches what the crop needs at each growth stage, rather than being applied once and forgotten.

  • Know the big three. Nitrogen (N) drives leafy growth, phosphorus (P) supports root and flower development, and potassium (K) strengthens overall plant health and disease resistance.
  • Split nitrogen applications. Applying nitrogen in two or three smaller doses — at planting, at knee-height, and around flowering for cereals — reduces losses to leaching and matches uptake to growth stage.
  • Don't neglect organic sources. Compost and manure release nutrients more slowly than mineral fertilizer but improve soil structure over time, making them a good complement rather than a substitute.
  • Watch for deficiency signs. Pale yellowing of older leaves often signals nitrogen shortage; purplish leaf tinges can indicate phosphorus deficiency, especially in cold or wet soils.
In the field Applying fertilizer to dry soil right before rain is more effective than applying it into standing water — nutrients can wash away before roots take them up.

05

Pest & Disease Management

Catching a problem early, while it's still confined to a few plants, is far cheaper and less damaging than treating a field-wide outbreak.

  • Scout regularly. Walk your field at least weekly, checking the undersides of leaves and growing points where pests like aphids and fall armyworm often hide first.
  • Identify before you treat. Not every insect is a pest — many are beneficial predators. Confirm what you're seeing before spraying, so you don't remove the pests' natural enemies along with them.
  • Use integrated methods. Combine cultural controls (crop rotation, resistant varieties, field sanitation), biological controls (beneficial insects) and chemical controls, using the least disruptive option that works.
  • Follow label rates and timing. Over-applying pesticide doesn't improve control, increases cost, harms beneficial insects and can lead to resistance building up in the pest population over time.
  • Manage disease through the environment. Many fungal diseases spread fastest in dense, poorly ventilated canopies — correct spacing and removing infected plant debris reduces pressure without a single spray.

06

Weed Control

Weeds compete directly with your crop for water, light and nutrients — the first four to six weeks after planting are the most critical window to keep a field clean.

  • Prioritize the early window. A field kept weed-free for the first month after emergence generally protects most of the potential yield, even if some weeds return later.
  • Combine methods. Hand weeding, hoeing, mulching and, where appropriate, herbicide each have a place — relying on just one method is usually more labor-intensive or less effective than combining two.
  • Mulch to suppress and conserve. A layer of crop residue or straw between rows blocks light to weed seedlings while also reducing soil moisture loss.
  • Don't let weeds go to seed. Removing weeds before they flower and set seed reduces the weed seed bank in your soil for future seasons, not just the current one.

07

Irrigation & Water Management

Whether you rely on rainfall or have access to irrigation, the goal is the same: water reaching the roots when the crop needs it most, without waste.

  • Know the critical stages. Water stress does the most damage during germination, flowering and grain-fill — if you can only irrigate at limited times, prioritize these windows.
  • Water deeply, less often. Frequent shallow watering encourages roots to stay near the surface, making plants more vulnerable to dry spells; deeper, less frequent watering encourages roots to grow down toward stored moisture.
  • Conserve with mulch and basins. Mulching reduces evaporation from the soil surface, and planting basins or tied ridges capture rainfall that would otherwise run off.
  • Check soil moisture directly. A simple finger or probe check 10–15cm down tells you more about whether it's time to irrigate than the number of days since it last rained.

08

Harvesting & Storage

A season's work can still be lost after a good harvest if timing, handling or storage go wrong — this stage deserves as much attention as planting.

  • Harvest at the right maturity. Grain crops are generally ready when kernels are hard and moisture content has dropped sufficiently; harvesting too early increases spoilage risk, too late increases losses to shattering, pests and weather.
  • Dry thoroughly before storage. Most grains need to be dried to a safe moisture level before storage — grain that feels dry to the hand can still be too wet to store safely, so where possible use a moisture meter or a simple salt test.
  • Clean and inspect storage containers. Residue and pests from the previous season's stock can infest a new harvest within weeks if storage bags, bins or granaries aren't cleaned out first.
  • Protect against storage pests. Weevils and grain borers cause some of the largest post-harvest losses — hermetic (airtight) bags, appropriate storage chemicals, or traditional smoke-cured granaries all reduce this risk.
In the field Post-harvest losses from poor drying and storage can rival losses from pests in the field — a lot of the "yield" farmers lose is lost after harvest, not before.

09

Improving Yields Over Time

The biggest yield gains usually come from small, consistent improvements season after season rather than one dramatic change.

  • Keep records. Note planting dates, varieties, input quantities, weather and final yield each season — over a few years, this reveals what actually works on your specific land.
  • Compare a small test plot. Before switching your whole field to a new variety, fertilizer program or spacing, trial it on a small section alongside your usual practice.
  • Rotate and rest strategically. Continuous cropping of the same species depletes specific nutrients and builds up pest and disease pressure — rotation and occasional fallow periods restore soil health.
  • Use available tools. Weather forecasts, soil sensors and farming apps can't replace field judgment, but they help you make better-timed decisions with less guesswork.
